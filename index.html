Step 2: Interactive Object Measurement

This browser application implements the same perspective-projection equations as measureObject.py. Both selected endpoints are assumed to lie at the entered depth from the camera.

Upload an image, then click the two endpoints of the dimension being measured.

The computation is:

X = (u − cx)Z/fx, Y = (v − cy)Z/fy, and D = √[(X₂ − X₁)² + (Y₂ − Y₁)²].

Module 3: Image Blurring — Spatial vs. Frequency-Domain Filtering

GitHub repository: CV_assignments — module3 folder

Image blurring is implemented two independent ways: (1) direct spatial convolution with a smoothing kernel, and (2) the Fourier-domain equivalent (multiply the image's FFT by the kernel's FFT, then inverse-transform). The demo below runs both, in the browser, on the same image and kernel, and reports the numerical difference between them.

Theory: Why They're the Same

The convolution theorem states that convolution in the spatial domain corresponds to multiplication in the frequency (Fourier) domain:

ℱ{f * h}(u, v) = F(u, v) · H(u, v)

Sketch of the derivation: take the Fourier transform of the convolution integral, swap the order of integration, substitute x′ = x − τ1, y′ = y − τ2 in the inner integral, and the inner integral reduces to H(u,v) times a phase factor depending only on (τ1, τ2) — which can then be pulled outside, leaving exactly F(u,v)·H(u,v). Full step-by-step derivation, including the discrete case and the zero-padding assumption needed to avoid circular-convolution wraparound artifacts, is in theory_convolution_theorem.md.

Experimental validation: across box and Gaussian filters at kernel sizes 3, 5, 9, 15, and 21, the mean absolute difference between the spatial and frequency-domain outputs was on the order of 10−14 — i.e. floating-point rounding error, not a real discrepancy, exactly as the theorem predicts. The in-browser demo above reports the same comparison live for whatever image and kernel you try.
